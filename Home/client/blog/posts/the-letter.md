No dashboard to babysit, no status meeting, and almost nothing to decide. In the Genosyn vision, the board's whole relationship with its company runs through one document: a letter from the AI CEO on the first of every month. Twenty minutes to read.

That puts a lot of weight on one letter. Here is what it owes the people who read it.

## The Goal comes first

A letter opens with progress toward the Goal, before revenue or anything else. What a company reports first tells its owners what it is really for. Sunwise's letters open with roofs, the Goal's own measure: 12 in the first month, then 1,100, then 6,200, on the way to a million.

## The bad news early

The second thing a letter owes is the truth about what went wrong, near the top, not buried in paragraph six. Sunwise's third-year letter says it plainly: "Our largest mistake came in March." Then what happened, who was affected, what it cost, and what changed so it will not happen again.

## Where the money went

What came in, what went out, what was swept into the reserves. In the vision, every figure traces back to the ledger, and an auditor the company cannot appoint checks it. A letter is not a press release. It is a report someone outside the company has verified.

## Almost nothing to decide

Most letters ask nothing. The company makes thousands of decisions a month on its own, and the letter reports the ones that matter. Once in a long while, a letter carries a question only an owner can answer: whether to sell the company, or what the next Goal should be. It arrives as a Decision, a question with options the company wrote, never a request for a rubber stamp. Across twenty years of Sunwise's letters, two ask anything at all.

## What ships toward it today

The monthly letter and its auditor are on the road, not in the product. The closest thing that ships today is a TLDR briefing. On a schedule, from every four hours to weekly, Genosyn writes a short recap of what the company did, drawn from its public channels, the Journal entries the company can see, and the output of finished Runs: one sentence and up to three bullets. Up to eight standing questions are answered after every briefing, so the questions an owner always asks get answered without being asked again.

Decisions ship too, much as the letter uses them: written by the AI Employee, with options it wrote, answerable by a Member, and never framed as a yes or no on something already done.

## Why a letter and not a dashboard

A dashboard asks you to watch. A letter asks you to read, once, and trust that it is complete. That is a higher bar for the company and a lower one for the owner, which is the right way round. The board's job is to own the company, not to monitor it.

> The whole truth, once a month: what happened, what went wrong, and where the money went.
